# How Countries Tax Wealth

Survey compiled 2026-08-06 from a multi-agent research pass with an adversarial
verification round on the load-bearing numbers (10 checks: 8 confirmed, 2 minor
corrections, applied below). Annual net wealth taxes only — distinct from
income, capital-gains, property and inheritance taxes.

The one-line history: 12 OECD countries levied net wealth taxes in 1990.
Three of that cohort survive (Switzerland, Norway, Spain). Nearly every exit
was driven by the same pair of problems: valuing wealth is hard, and wealth
moves. The two survivors that work — Switzerland and Norway — solved the
measurement problem in opposite ways, which is the most instructive fact in
this file.


---

## Currently levied

### Argentina
*Status: current, but being phased down under Milei (Law 27.743, June 2024): top rate falls each year to a flat 0.25% in 2027; optional REIBP prepayment lets taxpayers exit annual filing entirely through 2027*

**The tax:** Impuesto sobre los Bienes Personales — annual tax on personal assets held at Dec 31, in force since 1991. It is effectively a gross-asset tax: liabilities are generally NOT deductible (narrow exception tied to the family home).

**Rates & thresholds:** FY2025 (filed 2026): non-taxable minimum ARS 384,728,044.57 (CPI-indexed annually; roughly USD 300k at official rates); family home exempt up to a separate ARS 1,346,548,155.99. Progressive scale for 2025: 0.50% / 0.75% / 1.00% (PwC). Under Law 27.743 the top marginal rate steps down each year — 1.25% (2024), 1.00% (2025), 0.75% (2026) — converging to a SINGLE flat 0.25% rate in 2027. (Caveat: one secondary source, TaxAtlas, shows a slightly different path of 1.10% for 2025 / 1.00% for 2026 / 0.50% for 2027; the PwC schedule matching Law 27.743 is the better-supported version.) 'Good taxpayers' who were compliant 2020-2022 get an extra 0.50pp rate reduction through 2025. Non-residents pay a flat 0.5% on Argentine-situs assets via a substitute taxpayer. Shares in Argentine companies are taxed at the company level (responsable sustituto). MILEI CHANGES: Law 27.743 (Ley de Medidas Fiscales, companion to Ley Bases, June 2024) (1) abolished the previous punitive surcharge scale on FOREIGN-situs assets (which had reached 2.25% under the 2019 Fernández regime) — domestic and offshore assets now face one unified scale; (2) created the REIBP: a one-shot advance payment covering fiscal years 2023-2027 (0.45%/yr equivalent; 0.5% for amnesty-regularized assets) with no further wealth-tax filings until 2028 and fiscal stability on wealth-type taxes guaranteed until 2038; (3) ran the parallel 2024 'blanqueo' asset amnesty.

**How wealth is measured:** Residents taxed on WORLDWIDE assets. Domestic real estate at adjusted acquisition cost subject to a fiscal-valuation floor; vehicles per ARCA reference tables; foreign assets at market value converted at year-end FX. Exempt: Argentine savings accounts and term deposits, Argentine government bonds, and (up to its threshold) the family home — exemptions that heavily favor keeping wealth in domestic instruments.

**Revenue:** Bienes Personales was about 1.7% of total national tax collection in 2024 (ARCA annual report; ~0.4% of GDP given total tax pressure of ~23% of GDP per IARAF). 2024 collections were inflated by one-off REIBP prepayments; IARAF notes that excluding blanqueo/moratoria/REIBP extraordinary income, underlying revenue fell.

**Effects & rulings:** Argentina is the canonical wealth-tax-evasion case: decades of capital flight left a huge stock of undeclared offshore assets (the 2016-17 amnesty under Macri surfaced ~USD 117bn, widely reported at the time — figure from general reporting, not re-verified here). The Milei-era 2024 blanqueo regularized USD 32.15bn by end-2024 (USD 22.2bn cash into special CERA accounts, ~USD 10bn in real estate, vehicles and shareholdings) — verified via Infobae and El Cronista. The 2019-2023 offshore surcharge coincided with a documented exodus of wealthy Argentines shifting tax residency to Uruguay (aided by Uruguay's 2020 residency/tax-holiday incentives; Mercado Libre's Marcos Galperin is the emblematic case). The 2024 reform explicitly traded lower/converging rates plus prepayment stability for on-shoring assets.

### Bolivia
*Status: current as of Aug 2026, but slated for repeal: President Rodrigo Paz announced elimination Nov 25, 2025; the abrogation bill was rejected in a Chamber of Deputies committee in Feb 2026 and remains pending*

**The tax:** Impuesto a las Grandes Fortunas (IGF) — annual tax on large personal fortunes, Law 1357 of Dec 28, 2020, effective from 2021 (created under Luis Arce). Individuals only; companies are not subject.

**Rates & thresholds:** Applies to net fortune above Bs 30 million (~USD 4.3m at the official Bs 6.96/USD peg). Progressive marginal rates: 1.4% on Bs 30-40m; 1.9% on Bs 40-50m; 2.4% above Bs 50m. Resident individuals (183+ days/yr) are taxed on assets in Bolivia AND abroad (worldwide); non-resident individuals only on Bolivian-situs assets. Verified across Lexivox (law text), Wikipedia-es, Moreno Baldivieso, and impuestos.com.bo — consistent.

**How wealth is measured:** Annual self-assessed declaration to the Servicio de Impuestos Nacionales using regulatory reference values: real estate and vehicles at registry/cadastral-type tax values, financial assets at year-end balances, shareholdings at proportional net-equity value. Heavy reliance on self-reporting with a very thin audit base.

**Revenue:** Trivial: cumulative collections of ~Bs 785 million over 2021-2024 (~USD 113m total, i.e. under 0.1% of GDP per year); the taxpayer roll grew only from 204 to ~261 declarants nationwide. Sources: El País (Tarija), PPO Legal.

**Effects & rulings:** The near-empty taxpayer roll (~260 people declaring fortunes above Bs 30m in a country of 12m) is itself the headline evasion/avoidance fact. President Paz publicly blames the IGF for capital flight, saying such rules made Bolivia an investment 'island', and bundled its repeal with three other taxes (including the financial-transactions tax) in a Nov 2025 package to attract investment; the repeal stalled in the Deputies' economic-policy committee (Feb 2026) for lack of consensus, so the tax legally remains in force with taxpayers facing compliance risk if they stop filing (PPO Legal, Mar 2026).

### Colombia
*Status: current (permanent regime since 2023); Dec 2025 emergency expansion struck down by Constitutional Court in early 2026*

**The tax:** Impuesto al Patrimonio — permanent annual net wealth tax on individuals (Law 2277 of 2022, effective FY2023); foreign entities also pay on certain Colombian-situs assets not held through a branch

**Rates & thresholds:** Permanent regime, assessed on net wealth at Jan 1: threshold 72,000 UVT (COP 3,770,928,000 for 2026, roughly USD 900k-950k). Marginal rates: 0.5% on 72,000-122,000 UVT; 1.0% on 122,000-239,000 UVT; 1.5% above 239,000 UVT. The 1.5% top band applies only 2023-2026, dropping to 1.0% from 2027. EMERGENCY EPISODE: Legislative Decree 1474 (Dec 29, 2025, under a declared 'state of economic emergency' after Congress rejected Petro's tax reform) cut the threshold to 40,000 UVT (about COP 2.09bn / USD 500k) and added progressive rates up to 5% for wealth over 2,000,000 UVT — but the Constitutional Court (decision C-075 of 2026) declared the emergency declaration unconstitutional, so the decree fell and the ordinary Law 2277 regime governs 2026. Verified against PwC Worldwide Tax Summaries, KPMG TaxNewsFlash, Bolder Group, and IFC Review — all consistent.

**How wealth is measured:** Base is gross equity minus outstanding debts as of January 1. Assets are generally taken at their income-tax 'fiscal cost' (historic cost with tax adjustments), not market value — a structural undervaluation channel, especially for real estate and unlisted shares. First 12,000 UVT of the primary residence is excluded. Individuals self-report in the annual wealth-tax return (DIAN); filing deadline in May. Tax residents (183+ days in any 365-day window) are taxed on WORLDWIDE net wealth; non-residents only on Colombian-situs assets.

**Revenue:** Small: 32,118 individual payers in 2023 (0.5% of personal income-tax filers). All wealth-tax variants combined (including earlier corporate versions) raised about COP 49 trillion (~USD 12bn) over the 20 years to 2024 (La República); the current individuals-only tax yields well under 1% of total tax collections (DIAN total collections were COP ~278 trillion in 2023). Ministry of Finance projected the whole 2022 reform package for individuals (dividends + capital gains + wealth) at ~COP 3 trillion/yr.

**Effects & rulings:** Best-studied wealth tax in the developing world: Londoño-Vélez & Ávila-Mahecha (AER: Insights 2021, using leaked Panama Papers + DIAN microdata) found about two-fifths of the wealthiest 0.01% evaded, concealing roughly one-third of their wealth offshore; the top 0.01% were 24x more likely than the top 5% to be Mossack Fonseca clients; a 2015-17 voluntary disclosure scheme surfaced hidden wealth equal to ~1.7% of GDP; rate hikes trigger instant drops in reported wealth (bunching just below thresholds), erasing up to one-fifth of potential revenue. Recent flight signals: taxpayers in the top wealth band fell 28% between 2021 and 2024, and ~440 high-wealth individuals left Colombia between 2023 and 2024 (La Silla Vacía). The 2026 Constitutional Court ruling blocking the emergency expansion is itself a major documented event.

### Colombia
*Status: current*

**The tax:** Impuesto al Patrimonio — made a permanent annual net wealth tax by the Nov 2022 Petro tax reform (previously episodic/temporary); the only major Latin American economy with a permanent one (Argentina retains 'bienes personales')

**Rates & thresholds:** Applies above 72,000 UVT of net equity (~COP 3.0bn in 2023, roughly USD 800k–900k): 0.5% up to 122,000 UVT, 1.0% to 239,000 UVT, 1.5% above — the 1.5% top band is temporary for 2023–2026, dropping to 1.0% thereafter. First 12,000 UVT of primary residence excluded. Non-residents taxed on Colombian-situs assets only. A separate temporary wealth tax on corporations appeared in Feb 2026 (KPMG TaxNewsFlash).

**How wealth is measured:** Net equity (assets minus debts) measured each 1 January under tax-value rules; self-assessed within the income tax system with DIAN information reporting; Colombia participates in CRS exchange.

**Revenue:** Small single-digit share of revenue; Colombia's wealth tax has historically raised on the order of 0.1–0.3% of GDP (OECD/DIAN figures vary by year and by whether corporate variants are included).

**Effects & rulings:** Too recent for robust behavioral studies; Colombia is regularly cited in G20/UN discussions as evidence wealth taxes are spreading beyond rich countries.

Sources: <https://taxsummaries.pwc.com/colombia/individual/other-taxes> · <https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2022/11/novedades-sobre-el-impuesto-al-patrimonio-en-colombia> · <https://kpmg.com/us/en/taxnewsflash/news/2026/02/colombia-temporary-wealth-tax-corporations.html>

### France
*Status: current (introduced 2018 as the narrowed successor to the ISF)*

**The tax:** Impôt sur la fortune immobilière (IFI) — annual wealth tax on REAL ESTATE ONLY (directly or indirectly held, including via SCIs and property funds); financial wealth entirely exempt since 2018.

**Rates & thresholds:** Same scale and thresholds as the old ISF, unchanged at the switch: trigger at €1.3M of net taxable real-estate wealth, then 0.5% on €800k–1.3M; 0.7% €1.3–2.57M; 1% €2.57–5M; 1.25% €5–10M; 1.5% above €10M. The 75%-of-income cap is retained.

**How wealth is measured:** Self-assessed market value at 1 January, declared on the 2042-IFI annexes to the income-tax return. 30% abatement on the main residence. Property used in the owner's main professional activity exempt; property-related debts deductible subject to anti-abuse rules (e.g. interest-only loan amortisation deemed, restrictions on related-party loans). Indirect holdings valued via the property fraction of company/fund shares.

**Revenue:** €1.29bn in 2018, rising with property prices to €1.9bn in 2023 (176,000 households) and €2.2bn in 2024 (~186,000 households, +11% y/y) per DGFiP — versus the ISF's €4.2bn from ~358,000 households. Roughly 0.2% of French tax revenue.

**Effects & rulings:** Taxpayer count roughly halved versus ISF. France Stratégie's evaluation committee (2019–2023 reports): expatriations of wealthy taxpayers FELL after the 2017–18 reforms and returns rose — the net outflow of wealth-tax payers turned into a net inflow — but the committee stresses these are small numbers and no clear causal link can be established. Dividend payouts jumped by ~€10bn after the accompanying PFU flat tax (with 64% of dividends going to households declaring >€100k of dividends, vs 44% pre-reform); the committee found no measurable aggregate-investment boost attributable to the reform. Debate about partially re-broadening (e.g. the proposed 'Zucman tax') continues as high earners increasingly relocate for other tax reasons.

### Netherlands
*Status: current but twice ruled unlawful; narrowed by court-ordered redress (2021, 2024); replacement by an actual-return tax planned for 2028*

**The tax:** Box 3 of the income tax — a tax on a DEEMED (fictitious) return on net assets (savings, listed investments, second homes, crypto, receivables), not on actual income and not formally a wealth tax, though it functions as one.

**Rates & thresholds:** Flat 36% (2024–2026) on the deemed return. 2025 deemed-return percentages: 1.44% on savings/bank balances, 5.88% on investments and other assets, 2.62% deduction rate on debts; tax-free capital €57,684 per person (€115,368 per fiscal couple). Effective burden on investment assets ≈ 2.1% of their value (36% × 5.88%). For 2026 the government proposed raising the other-assets forfait to 7.78% and cutting the allowance to €51,396, but parliament amended the Belastingplan 2026: the forfait becomes 6.00% and the allowance €59,357 instead (bank-balance forfait provisionally ~1.28%).

**How wealth is measured:** Single snapshot of assets and debts at 1 January each year. Reporting is largely automatic: Dutch banks, brokers and insurers must report balances/portfolio values to the Belastingdienst, which pre-fills the return; real estate is valued at the municipal WOZ value (rented dwellings get a leegwaarderatio discount); the savings forfait is set retroactively from actual average deposit rates. Since the June 2024 ruling, taxpayers may instead prove their ACTUAL return was lower (Opgaaf Werkelijk Rendement form, available July 2025, part of the regular return from 2026) — but 'actual return' includes unrealised value changes, with no tax-free allowance and no cost deduction in the comparison.

**Revenue:** Roughly €4–5bn/year (each year's delay of the reform is officially costed at ~€2.4–2.5bn/year in lost/foregone revenue) — on the order of 1% of Dutch tax revenue. Court-ordered redress for 2017–2022 savers cost ~€3bn, and the 2024 ruling adds billions more in refunds.

**Effects & rulings:** Supreme Court 'Christmas judgment' of 24 December 2021: the post-2017 deemed-return system violates the ECHR (right to property + non-discrimination) where deemed exceeds actual return — mass-objection refunds followed. Supreme Court 6 June 2024: the repair laws (Herstelwet 2017–2022 and Overbruggingswet from 2023) STILL violate the ECHR; the state must reduce assessments to actual return on request. Reform slipped 2025 → 2026 → 2027 → 2028: the 'Wet werkelijk rendement box 3' (final bill published; entry 1 Jan 2028) taxes actual returns — realised income plus unrealised value changes for most assets, realisation basis for real estate and startup shares, planned rate ~36%. Documented behavioural effects: large-scale shifting of savings/investments into Box 2 holding companies (spaargeld-BV) and a wave of litigation; the fiscal mess is court-driven rather than emigration-driven.

### Norway
*Status: current*

**The tax:** Formuesskatt — national annual net wealth tax (levied since 1892), split between municipal and state components, on worldwide net wealth of residents

**Rates & thresholds:** 2025 income year: total 1.0% = 0.525% municipal + 0.475% state on net wealth above NOK 1,760,000 (single) / NOK 3,520,000 (married couples, combined); total 1.1% above NOK 20,700,000 (state component rises to 0.575%). 2026: totals unchanged at 1.0%/1.1% but the split shifts — municipal cut to 0.35%, state raised to 0.65% (tier 1) and 0.75% (tier 2); thresholds raised to NOK 1,900,000 / NOK 3,800,000, second tier to NOK 21,500,000. New from 2026: permanent deferral scheme — taxpayers can apply to postpone payment up to 3 years if wealth tax exceeds NOK 30,000. Verified against PwC Tax Summaries, Skatteetaten, Aider Legal 2026-budget note, and Life in Norway

**How wealth is measured:** Worldwide gross assets minus debt, with statutory valuation discounts: PRIMARY HOME valued at 25% of estimated market value up to NOK 10m, and 70% of the value above NOK 10m (i.e. only 30% discount on the excess); SECONDARY HOMES at 100% of market value since 2023; SHARES (listed and unlisted, including commercial property held via companies) at 80% of value — a 20% discount; bank deposits and cash at 100%. Home 'market values' are set by a Statistics Norway hedonic model, not individual appraisal. Debt allocated to discounted assets is deductible only at the same reduced percentage (e.g. debt attributed to shares deductible at 80%). Reporting: extensive THIRD-PARTY reporting — banks and financial institutions automatically report account balances, loans and holdings; employers report pay; the shareholder register reports equity positions; tax returns arrive PRE-FILLED and the taxpayer only corrects/adds (opposite of the Swiss model)

**Revenue:** NOK 32 billion in 2023 (~$3.3bn) from 671,639 taxpayers (Reuters; ~12% of the population); estimated NOK 34bn for 2025, up from NOK 27bn in 2022 — revenues rose despite the exodus. As a share: ~1.1% of total tax revenue and ~0.4% of GDP circa 2019 (Wealth Tax Commission, Banoun); currently roughly 1% of total tax revenue given Norway's very large overall tax take

**Effects & rulings:** Well-documented emigration wave after the 2021-elected centre-left government raised the rate from 0.85% to 1.0%/1.1% and cut valuation discounts: at least 30 wealthy Norwegians left in 2022 alone, including Aker billionaire Kjell Inge Røkke (moved to Lugano; had paid NOK 181m tax the prior year); 82 rich Norwegians with combined wealth ~NOK 46bn left in 2022-23; Civita (think-tank) counts 261 residents with assets over NOK 10m leaving in 2022 and 254 in 2023 — more than double the pre-hike rate; by 2024 some reports put ~300 Norwegian multimillionaires/billionaires in Switzerland, the overwhelmingly favoured destination. Estimated lost annual wealth-tax revenue NOK 3-5bn, though total wealth-tax receipts still grew (27bn 2022 to ~34bn 2025 est.). Response: exit tax sharply tightened in 2024 — deemed disposal at 37.8% on unrealised share gains above NOK 3m on emigration, payable immediately or over 12 years with interest (the old indefinite deferral loophole closed); further tightening proposed. Wealth tax was a central issue in the September 2025 election; Labour won and the tax stays (poll: 39% keep/raise, 23% cut, 28% abolish). No major court rulings against the tax itself

### Norway
*Status: current*

**The tax:** Formuesskatt — national net wealth tax split between state and municipalities on residents' worldwide net assets

**Rates & thresholds:** 2025–2026: 1.0% total (0.35% municipal + 0.65% state) on net wealth above NOK 1.9 million (~€165k; doubled for couples), rising to 1.1% (0.35% + 0.75%) above NOK 21.5 million. Threshold raised from NOK 1.76m in 2025 to 1.9m in 2026 (confirmed on skatteetaten.no).

**How wealth is measured:** The opposite of Switzerland on reporting: near-fully third-party. Banks, employers, and the securities registry report directly to Skatteetaten and returns arrive pre-filled. But the base is riddled with statutory valuation discounts: primary home counted at 25% of estimated market value up to NOK 10m and 70% of the value above that; secondary homes at 100% since 2023; listed/unlisted shares and operating assets have carried a ~20% valuation discount (80% inclusion) in recent years; unlisted companies valued at the company's fiscal net-asset value (book-based, typically well below market). These discounts drive portfolio distortion toward housing.

**Revenue:** ~NOK 34 billion estimated for 2025, up from ~NOK 27bn in 2022 and ~NOK 15bn in 2021. Roughly 0.4–0.6% of GDP and ~1.1–1.5% of total tax revenue (OECD 4300 basis; the share has risen with the 2022 hikes).

**Effects & rulings:** Best-documented emigration episode: after the 2022 rate/valuation increases, think-tank Civita counted 261 residents with wealth above NOK 10m leaving in 2022 and 254 in 2023 (more than double the pre-hike rate); business magazine Kapital reports 105 of Norway's 400 richest now live abroad or have shifted wealth to relatives abroad (Fredriksen, Røkke among high-profile departures, mostly to Switzerland). Norway responded by tightening the exit tax: 37.8% on unrealized gains above NOK 3m, with the indefinite-deferral loophole closed in 2024 (payment within 12 years). Around 40% of emigrants are business owners. Yet revenue kept rising — the base is broad, so the exodus did not dent collections. A separate liquidity critique (owners forced to take taxable dividends to pay the tax on illiquid company stakes) is central to the domestic debate; the 2025 election kept the tax in place with a government-appointed committee reviewing it.

### Spain
*Status: current (state law; regionally rebated to zero in some regions)*

**The tax:** Impuesto sobre el Patrimonio (IP) — annual net wealth tax on worldwide assets of residents (Spanish-situs assets of non-residents); revenue and rate-setting devolved to the 17 autonomous communities.

**Rates & thresholds:** State default scale: 8 brackets from 0.2% to 3.5% (top rate applies above €10,695,996; raised from 2.5% to 3.5% in 2021). Tax-free allowance €700,000 per person plus up to €300,000 exemption on the primary residence, so a resident typically pays nothing below ~€1M. Regions override: Madrid and Andalucía apply a 100% bonificación (effective rate 0%), Extremadura 100% since 2023, Galicia 50% rebate; Catalonia uses a €500,000 allowance and top rate 3.48% (above €20M); Valencia allowance €500,000 and top rate 3.5%. Joint cap: wealth tax + income tax cannot exceed 60% of taxable income, but the wealth-tax bill can be reduced by at most 80% (a minimum 20% is always due); the cap does not apply to non-residents.

**How wealth is measured:** Self-assessed annual return (Modelo 714, filed with the June income-tax campaign), snapshot at 31 December. Real estate: the HIGHEST of cadastral value, value determined/checked by the administration for another tax, or acquisition price — since 2022 the new cadastral 'valor de referencia' effectively floors valuations for post-2022 purchases. Bank accounts/deposits: the higher of the 31-Dec balance and the average balance of the fourth quarter. Listed shares: average trading price of the fourth quarter (official Q4 list published by the tax agency); funds at year-end NAV. Unlisted companies: greatest of nominal value, theoretical book value per the last approved balance sheet, or capitalisation of average profits. Key exemptions: qualifying family business assets/shareholdings ('empresa familiar' — activity, ≥5%/20% family participation, management-remuneration tests), pension funds, household contents.

**Revenue:** IP alone raises roughly €1.2–1.4bn/year; IP + solidarity tax combined ~€1.98bn in 2023, with ~190,000 filers declaring close to €1tn of wealth. That is on the order of 0.5% of total Spanish tax revenue — small despite the political weight.

**Effects & rulings:** Madrid's 100% rebate (in place since the tax's 2011 reactivation) made it an internal wealth-tax haven — wealthy taxpayers concentrated there; Andalucía copied it in 2022. The central government answered with the ITSGF top-up (below). Spain's Constitutional Court upheld the ITSGF in November 2023 against challenges from Madrid/Andalucía. Madrid then re-established its own wealth tax for taxpayers who would owe ITSGF anyway, purely so the money stays regional — behaviourally, the rebate war became a revenue-allocation war rather than tax relief.

### Spain
*Status: current (created as 'temporary' for 2022–2023 by Law 38/2022; extended indefinitely by RDL 8/2023 pending regional-financing reform)*

**The tax:** Impuesto Temporal de Solidaridad de las Grandes Fortunas (ITSGF) — state-level 'solidarity' top-up wealth tax on net wealth above €3M, designed explicitly to neutralise the Madrid/Andalucía 100% rebates.

**Rates & thresholds:** 0% up to €3,000,000; 1.7% on €3,000,000–5,347,998; 2.1% on €5,347,998–10,695,996; 3.5% above €10,695,996. Residents also apply the €700,000 personal allowance, so the effective entry point is ~€3.7M. Regional wealth tax actually paid is fully credited against ITSGF, so it only bites where the regional tax is rebated. The same 60%-of-income cap (min 20% payable) applies to residents.

**How wealth is measured:** Identical base and valuation rules to the ordinary wealth tax (same 31-December snapshot, same real-estate/bank/Q4-share rules, same family-business and primary-residence exemptions); separately self-assessed via Modelo 718 in July.

**Revenue:** €623M in 2023 (about 12,010 payers, ~89% from Madrid); collapsed to ~€38M in 2024 after Madrid and other rebate regions reinstated their own wealth taxes to capture the credit. First-year yield was roughly 0.1–0.2% of Spanish tax revenue.

**Effects & rulings:** Upheld by the Constitutional Court in November 2023 (challenges by Madrid, Andalucía and others rejected). Its main documented effect was fiscal, not behavioural: it forced rebate regions to reactivate their wealth taxes, shifting revenue from the central ITSGF to regional IP (hence the €623M→€38M collapse). Around 23,000 people were initially estimated liable. Announced as a measure against 'tax-haven' competition between regions rather than a big revenue raiser.

### Spain
*Status: current*

**The tax:** Two-layer system: (1) regional Impuesto sobre el Patrimonio (wealth tax, devolved to autonomous communities) and (2) the state 'Solidarity Tax on Large Fortunes' (ITSGF), enacted late 2022 as 'temporary' for 2022–23 precisely to override regional zero-rating (Madrid), then extended indefinitely in Dec 2023 pending wealth-tax reform

**Rates & thresholds:** Wealth tax: state default scale 0.2%–3.5% above a €700,000 exemption plus €300,000 primary-home exemption; regions set their own scales/reliefs (Madrid had 100% relief). ITSGF: 1.7% on net wealth €3m–5.35m, 2.1% on €5.35m–10.7m, 3.5% above €10.7m, with a credit for regional wealth tax paid — so it only bites where regions zero-rate. A ceiling limits combined income tax + wealth tax to 60% of taxable income (minimum 20% of the wealth tax still due). Basque Country and Navarre excluded (own regimes).

**How wealth is measured:** Self-assessment, but backstopped by heavy information reporting: real estate at the highest of cadastral, acquisition, or administratively assessed value; listed securities at average Q4 market price; unlisted shares at the highest of nominal, book, or capitalized-earnings value; Modelo 720 requires declaration of foreign assets, and CRS feeds the AEAT. Certain business assets, pension rights, and some heritage/art items are exempt.

**Revenue:** ITSGF first collection (July 2023, for 2022): €623 million — well below estimates — of which Madrid alone €555m. Combined net wealth taxes: ~0.19% of GDP and ~0.51% of total tax revenue in 2022 (OECD 4300 basis).

**Effects & rulings:** Constitutional Court rejected Madrid's challenge to the ITSGF on 7 Nov 2023. Game-theoretic response: Madrid, Cantabria, Extremadura (and Andalusia) then restored their own wealth taxes so the revenue stays regional instead of flowing to the central ITSGF — the solidarity tax's main effect was ending regional tax competition to zero, not raising central revenue. The 'Spain-style solidarity tax' is the template most often cited by campaigners elsewhere.

### Switzerland
*Status: current*

**The tax:** Cantonal/communal annual net wealth tax (no federal wealth tax) — levied by all 26 cantons since the 19th century and mandatory under the federal Tax Harmonisation Act; the world's longest-running net wealth tax regime

**Rates & thresholds:** Rates and allowances vary by canton and commune. Effective top rates range from roughly 0.13% (Nidwalden, flat cantonal schedule of 0.25 per mille; ~0.13% effective on CHF 5m after multipliers) to ~0.9-1.0% (Geneva, top statutory bracket 4.5 per mille plus 1.35 per mille supplementary tax = 4.85 per mille before communal add-ons; ~0.9% effective on CHF 5m). Zurich: exemption CHF 80,000 single / ~CHF 159,000 married (2026), progressive schedule from 0.05% rising to 0.30% marginal on wealth above CHF 3,304,000. Geneva: allowance CHF 82,200 per adult + CHF 41,100 per child. Obwalden and Schwyz also near the bottom with flat-rate systems. Typical tax-free thresholds ~CHF 80,000 single / ~CHF 160,000 married; verified against PwC Tax Summaries, Taxolution, and The Poor Swiss

**How wealth is measured:** Worldwide net assets: bank balances, securities, funds, life-insurance surrender values, vehicles, boats, real estate, art/jewellery. Foreign real estate and foreign business assets are exempt but count for rate progression. Exempt: household goods, and crucially ALL pension assets (pillar 2 occupational and pillar 3a) until withdrawal. Listed securities valued at Dec 31 market price per the Federal Tax Administration's official Kursliste; unlisted companies under Federal Circular 28 (mix of net asset value and capitalised earnings). Real estate enters at the cantonal 'tax value', typically only 60-80% of market price depending on canton. Debts fully deductible, no cap. Reporting: pure SELF-declaration — Swiss banks do NOT report Swiss residents' accounts to Swiss tax authorities (domestic banking secrecy, Banking Act Art. 47, remains intact; AEOI reporting applies only to foreign-resident account holders). Banks prepare tax statements for the client, not the authority

**Revenue:** Highest wealth-tax reliance in the OECD: 1.1% of GDP and 3.9% of total tax revenue in 2018 (CEPR/Brülhart et al.); OECD Revenue Statistics show 1.19% of GDP and ~4.35% of total tax revenue in 2022; secondary sources cite a 3.6-4.9% range depending on year — an order of magnitude above any other country despite low rates, because thresholds are low and the base is broad

**Effects & rulings:** Landmark study Brülhart, Gruber, Krapf & Schmidheiny (AEJ: Economic Policy, 2022): a 1 percentage-point cut in a canton's wealth tax rate raises reported taxable wealth by at least 43% after 6 years — about 24% of the effect from taxpayer mobility between cantons, 21% from concurrent housing-price rises, with the large residual attributed mainly to reporting/evasion responses in a system with no third-party reporting of financial wealth. Strong inter-cantonal tax competition (wealthy cluster in Nidwalden/Zug/Schwyz). Switzerland is itself the main destination for wealth-tax emigrants from Norway. No significant court rulings against the tax; it enjoys stable political acceptance and cantons are federally required to levy it

### Switzerland
*Status: current*

**The tax:** Cantonal/communal net wealth tax (Vermögenssteuer) — the oldest continuously operating broad wealth tax; levied by all 26 cantons on residents' worldwide net assets, no federal component

**Rates & thresholds:** Top combined cantonal+communal rates roughly 0.1%–1.0% depending on canton and commune (Zurich tops out at ~0.30% above CHF 3,304,000 in 2026; Geneva is near the ~1% high end, Zug/Nidwalden at the low end). Exempt thresholds are low by international standards (roughly CHF 50k–250k depending on canton), so the tax reaches the upper middle class, not just the rich.

**How wealth is measured:** Pure self-declaration: taxpayers list worldwide assets and debts on the annual return. Critically, there is NO domestic third-party reporting of financial wealth — Swiss banks do not report residents' accounts to Swiss tax authorities (Switzerland sends CRS data abroad but retains domestic bank secrecy vs. its own tax offices). Listed securities at market value; unlisted companies valued by the federal 'Praktikermethode' (Kreisschreiben 28: weighted average of capitalized earnings and net asset value), which typically undervalues; real estate at cantonal tax values usually below market; household effects (often including art held as furnishings) exempt.

**Revenue:** The outlier: ~1.19% of GDP and ~4.35% of total tax revenue in 2022 (OECD category 4300, cited by Tax Foundation) — by far the highest in the OECD, reflecting low thresholds, low rates, and the absence of most other capital taxes (no federal capital gains tax on private securities).

**Effects & rulings:** Brülhart, Gruber, Krapf & Schmidheiny (AEJ: Economic Policy 2022), using cantonal variation: a 1pp cut in the wealth tax rate raises reported wealth by at least 43% after 6 years; ~24% of the response is taxpayer mobility, ~20% house-price capitalization, and the large remainder is consistent with sizable evasion in this no-third-party-reporting setting. Despite this, the tax is politically stable and Switzerland is the destination of choice for wealth-tax emigrants from Norway and France.

### Uruguay
*Status: current (long-standing; personal rates deliberately phased down to near-nominal levels since the 2007 reform)*

**The tax:** Impuesto al Patrimonio (IP) — annual net wealth tax on assets LOCATED IN URUGUAY at Dec 31 for individuals, family units, and undivided estates; a parallel flat corporate IP (1.5% of net fiscal assets, higher for banks) is where the real revenue is.

**Rates & thresholds:** Individuals, 2026: non-taxable minimum UYU 6,653,000 (~USD 165k), doubled to UYU 13,306,000 for a family-unit filing. Resident individuals (and non-residents who are subject to IRNR) pay a flat 0.1% on the excess. Non-residents NOT subject to IRNR face an older progressive scale of 0.7%-1.5%. Companies pay 1.5% flat with no threshold. A different (higher) threshold applies to agricultural assets. Verified across PwC, DGI-linked calculators (calculame.uy), and Global Tax Uruguay — consistent.

**How wealth is measured:** Strictly TERRITORIAL — worldwide assets of residents are NOT taxed, only Uruguayan-situs assets (a key reason Uruguay markets itself to relocating HNWIs). Real estate at cadastral value (typically far below market); household goods added as a fixed percentage presumption; vehicles at registry values; many personal financial assets are exempt or outside the base. Deductible liabilities for individuals are essentially limited to the annual average of debts with local banks. Self-assessed via annual DGI return (individuals' valuation date Dec 31; companies at balance-sheet close).

**Revenue:** The IP as a whole is a significant revenue source by world wealth-tax standards — on the order of 1% of GDP (OECD Revenue Statistics category; approximate, not pinned to a single year in this research) — but the overwhelming share comes from the 1.5% corporate levy; the 0.1% personal wealth tax raises very little (no individual/company split was published in the sources found; DGI reports would be needed for exact figures).

**Effects & rulings:** No dramatic evasion story because the personal tax is designed to be light: cadastral undervaluation, exempt financial assets, the 0.1% rate, and territorial scope hollow out the individual base by construction. The documented behavioural story runs the other way: Uruguay actively ATTRACTED wealthy taxpayers fleeing Argentina's wealth tax (2020 relaxation of tax-residency rules plus a 10-11 year income-tax holiday for new residents), and the territorial IP means their foreign wealth stays untaxed. Main policy debate is the corporate IP's drag on capital-intensive business, not personal evasion.

## Narrowed

### France
*Status: narrowed(2018); Zucman-style 2% floor proposed and blocked (2025); new 20% holding-company asset tax enacted (2026)*

**The tax:** Broad ISF (Impôt de Solidarité sur la Fortune) repealed in 2018, replaced by IFI (Impôt sur la Fortune Immobilière) covering only real estate. Frontier development: the national 'taxe Zucman' (2% minimum on wealth ≥ €100m) was adopted by the National Assembly in February 2025 but blocked by the Senate months later. The compromise in the Finance Act for 2026 (adopted 2 Feb 2026, upheld by the Conseil constitutionnel 19 Feb 2026) is instead a 20% tax on 'non-productive' assets held inside holding companies.

**Rates & thresholds:** IFI: progressive to 1.5% max on real estate above €1.3m. New 2026 holding tax: 20% on non-professional ('extravagant'/non-productive) assets in closely held holding companies where asset FMV exceeds €5m, a family circle holds ≥50%, and passive income exceeds 50% of income; targets yachts, private jets, leisure vehicles etc. held in wrappers — but explicitly EXCLUDES art, collectibles and antiques. Blocked Zucman version: 2% annual floor on total wealth ≥ €100m, income taxes paid creditable.

**How wealth is measured:** IFI: self-assessed market value of real estate (30% abatement on the primary residence). The holding-company tax is an anti-abuse response to the core Zucman diagnosis: French billionaires accumulate returns untaxed inside holding companies, so their personal taxable income — and effective tax rate — is tiny relative to wealth.

**Revenue:** IFI raises ~€2bn/yr (~0.07% of GDP), versus ~€4–5bn from the old ISF. The blocked Zucman tax was scored by supporters at ~€15–20bn/yr from ~1,800 households.

**Effects & rulings:** ISF repeal is the canonical 'wealth taxes fail' exhibit (capital flight estimates, ~40+ year running debate); the 2025 Assembly vote made France the first G7 legislature to pass a Zucman-style floor, and its Senate defeat plus the diluted 2026 outcome is the clearest measure of where the political frontier actually is. Macron's government backs the idea internationally at the G20 while resisting the full domestic version.

### Luxembourg
*Status: narrowed(2006) — abolished for individuals from 1 Jan 2006; retained to this day for opaque companies*

**The tax:** Impôt sur la fortune — annual net wealth tax; the individual leg was scrapped, the corporate leg survives

**Rates & thresholds:** Individuals (pre-2006): 0.5% of net wealth. Companies (current): 0.5% on net assets up to €500m, 0.05% on the excess, with minimum NWT brackets (roughly €535 up to €32,100 depending on balance sheet).

**How wealth is measured:** Balance-sheet net asset value at year-end for companies (worldwide assets for residents, Luxembourg assets for non-residents); individual wealth had been self-declared — weakly enforceable in a bank-secrecy financial center.

**Revenue:** Individual portion was small (not separately material in revenue statistics); the corporate NWT remains a real revenue source today. No reliable two-source final-year figure for the individual tax — flagging that.

**Effects & rulings:** Stated rationale: private-banking/financial-center competitiveness amid the EU Savings Directive era; abolition for individuals was packaged with a new 10% final withholding tax on residents' interest income. The structural lesson: the tax survived only where assessment is trivially easy (audited corporate balance sheets) and died where it depended on individuals self-reporting.

### Netherlands
*Status: narrowed(2024) by courts; replacement regime pending*

**The tax:** Box 3 — not a formal net wealth tax but an income tax on a DEEMED return on net assets (excluding primary residence and pensions), taxed at 36%; economically equivalent to a ~1.5–2% wealth tax on investment assets. Repeatedly struck down: the Supreme Court's 2021 'Christmas ruling' and again on 6 June 2024 held the deemed-return system violates ECHR property/non-discrimination rights whenever the deemed return exceeds the actual return.

**Rates & thresholds:** 36% on a presumed return (~6% presumed for investments, lower for savings) on net assets above ~€57k per person. Post-ruling, taxpayers can file an 'actual return' form (Opgaaf Werkelijk Rendement, available since July 2025) covering realized AND unrealized gains, nominal, no loss carryover.

**How wealth is measured:** Fully third-party-reported financial assets (banks/brokers report to the Belastingdienst); the litigation was about the fictional RETURN, not asset values — a cautionary tale that presumptive approaches to taxing wealth can fail on human-rights/property grounds. A statutory actual-return system is planned (target 2027–2028).

**Revenue:** Box 3 raises roughly €4–5bn/yr (order of magnitude; shrinking under court-ordered repair).

**Effects & rulings:** Billions in compensation/repair costs to the Treasury; the case is cited across Europe as the legal risk of formula-based wealth taxation. The original Dutch net wealth tax proper was repealed in 2001.

## Proposed / debated

### G20 / Global (Zucman 2% billionaire minimum)
*Status: proposed — declaratory consensus 2024, expert-track continuation 2025, stalled under 2026 US presidency*

**The tax:** Zucman blueprint (commissioned by Brazil's G20 presidency, presented July 2024): a coordinated MINIMUM — individuals with >$1bn (some versions >$100m) must pay total tax of at least 2% of wealth per year, with income taxes paid creditable, so it functions as a top-up, not a classic wealth tax. Estimated $200–250bn/yr from ~3,000 people. Milestones: July 2024 Rio finance-ministers' declaration — first-ever G20 commitment to 'engage cooperatively to ensure ultra-high-net-worth individuals are effectively taxed'; Nov 2024 leaders' declaration (best practices, anti-avoidance, no binding mechanism); 2025 South Africa presidency continued via a Stiglitz-led expert taskforce and a follow-up blueprint (Tax Justice Network tracking); the US dismissed the initiative and, with the 2026 US G20 presidency, the multilateral track is effectively parked — action has shifted to national experiments (France, California) and the UN tax convention process.

**Rates & thresholds:** 2% of wealth floor above $1bn; Zucman's book/report also models $100m thresholds and 3% variants.

**How wealth is measured:** The blueprint's core administrative claim: at the billionaire level wealth is mostly listed equity (easy to mark to market); unlisted stakes via formula/latest-round values; enforcement rides on CRS-style exchange plus a proposed exit/'tax the leaver' backstop, deliberately mimicking the Pillar Two minimum-tax architecture.

**Revenue:** Hypothetical $200–250bn/yr globally (Zucman estimate), roughly doubling current effective billionaire tax rates of ~0.3% of wealth.

**Effects & rulings:** Rebuttal literature (Intertax 2026 debate; ICTD) argues capacity-limited tax administrations should use existing tools first; Elcano/UNRISD papers analyze revenue durability given billionaire wealth dynamics.

### United Kingdom
*Status: proposed/debated — not adopted (Nov 2025 Budget declined it)*

**The tax:** No annual wealth tax; the debate is the loudest in Europe. Patriotic Millionaires UK / Tax Justice UK campaign for a 2% annual tax on individual wealth over £10 million (claimed ~£24bn/yr; part of a 10-reform package claimed at £50–60bn). Over 120 millionaires signed a July 2026 letter demanding higher wealth taxes; polling cited: 75% of the public and 80% of UK millionaires support the wealthy paying more. Chancellor Reeves's November 2025 Budget instead raised ~£26bn by 2030-31 through other measures (threshold freezes etc.) and did not introduce a wealth tax. The 2020 Wealth Tax Commission (LSE/Warwick) remains the reference analysis: it favored a one-off wealth tax and argued against an annual one on administrability grounds.

**Rates & thresholds:** Campaign proposal: 2% > £10m. Wealth Tax Commission one-off benchmark: 5% over £500k paid over 5 years (~£260bn).

**How wealth is measured:** UK-specific obstacles rehearsed in the debate: no register of wealth, heavy reliance on valuations for private businesses/farmland (fresh from the inheritance-tax farm dispute), and non-dom abolition (Apr 2025) already testing HNW mobility.

**Revenue:** n/a (hypothetical £24bn/yr claim for the 2%/£10m design, campaign-modelled)

**Effects & rulings:** Reported non-dom/HNW departures after the 2024–25 reforms are the counter-campaign's main exhibit (contested numbers); government position is that CGT/IHT reform, not an annual wealth tax, is the vehicle.

### United States — California
*Status: proposed — Proposition 40 on the 3 Nov 2026 ballot*

**The tax:** 'One-Time Wealth Tax for State-Funded Health Care Programs' (2026 Billionaire Tax Act): a ONE-TIME 5% tax on the net worth of California-resident billionaires, earmarked for health care, food assistance and education. Qualified for the ballot 17 June 2026 (>875,000 valid signatures projected). The single biggest live wealth-tax vote in the world right now.

**Rates & thresholds:** 5% of worldwide net worth above $1 billion, measured as of 31 Dec 2026 for residents on 1 Jan 2026, payable in 2027 or in five 1%/yr installments with a deferral charge. Base includes businesses, stocks, bonds, art, collectibles, IP; EXCLUDES directly held real estate, pensions and retirement accounts.

**How wealth is measured:** Self-assessed with Franchise Tax Board audit; because the target is ~200 people whose wealth is mostly listed equity, proponents argue valuation is tractable; opponents (Tax Foundation, Pillsbury/SeeSALT) argue private-company and IP valuation plus the residency lookback invite decade-long litigation, and that taxing people who left before the vote raises due-process/commerce-clause issues.

**Revenue:** Prospective: ~$100bn total over 2027–31 per LAO central estimate (~5% of annual state budget per year while it runs).

**Effects & rulings:** LAO: ~$100bn over 5 years if billionaires stay; if all ~200 left, California loses ~$900m/yr of income tax (<1% of collections). ITEP/Saez-Galle-Gamage-Shanske expert report (Dec 2025) defends revenue and constitutionality; five major business groups filed opposition Dec 2025; Rep. Kiley introduced a federal bill against it. Widely framed (CS Monitor, July 2026) as the national test case for wealth taxation.

### United States — federal
*Status: proposed (dormant); constitutional question expressly open*

**Effects & rulings:** Federal inaction is why the action moved to states (CA Prop 40, WA) and to the G20/UN track.

## Repealed — the base-rate evidence

### Austria
*Status: repealed(1994)*

**The tax:** Vermögensteuer — annual net wealth tax on individuals AND corporations; abolished in the 1994 tax reform by SPÖ finance minister Ferdinand Lacina (a social democrat abolishing it is itself notable)

**Rates & thresholds:** 1% of net wealth (individuals and legal persons), low personal allowances.

**How wealth is measured:** Two structural failures: (1) real estate valued at badly outdated Einheitswerte (unit values), like Germany; (2) strict bank secrecy plus fully anonymous numbered accounts (legal until 2002) made private financial wealth effectively unreportable and unenforceable — so the burden fell mostly on companies' visible balance-sheet capital, making it in practice a tax on corporate investment.

**Revenue:** 1993 (last full year): ~0.47% of GDP / 1.12% of total tax revenue, down from 0.55% / 1.66% in 1965. Highest revenue share of the repealer group, but majority paid by corporations, not rich households.

**Effects & rulings:** Repeal was revenue-compensated by raising the corporate income tax rate from 30% to 34%, framed as swapping a distortionary tax on capital stock for a tax on profits. No significant post-repeal empirical study; the stated reasons were enforcement impossibility for private wealth and harm to corporate capital formation.

Sources: <https://case-research.eu/app/uploads/2026/04/wealth-taxation-including-net-wealth-capital-and-exit-KP0126015ENN.pdf> · <https://www.schoenherr.eu/content/re-introducing-wealth-taxes-in-austria-practical-challenges> · <https://www.politifact.com/factchecks/2019/may/10/joel-griffith/some-countries-ditched-wealth-taxes-what-might-mea> · <https://www.wealthandpolicy.com/wp/EP6_PoliticsAndDesign.pdf>

### Denmark
*Status: repealed(1997) — phased decision taken 1995, fully gone by 1997, under Social Democratic PM Poul Nyrup Rasmussen*

**The tax:** Formueskat — annual net wealth tax dating to 1903, one of the world's oldest; threshold sat around the 98th percentile of household wealth

**Rates & thresholds:** 2.2% flat above the threshold until 1988 (an extremely high rate on a stock — equivalent to confiscating a large share of normal returns); 1989 reform halved it to 1% and doubled the exemption threshold; ceiling rules capped combined income+wealth tax as a share of income (lowered from 78% to 68% in 1989).

**How wealth is measured:** Total household net wealth excluding pension wealth: cash, deposits, bonds, equities, housing, large durables and business assets, net of debt. Denmark's population registers meant most financial wealth was third-party reported; housing entered at public property valuations below market. Pension exclusion was the big base hole.

**Revenue:** Small by the 1990s: on the order of 0.1–0.2% of GDP (well under 0.5% of total tax revenue in a ~48%-of-GDP tax state). Note it had been the LARGEST wealth tax of its kind through the 1980s before the 1989 cuts.

**Effects & rulings:** The best-measured case in the world. Jakobsen, Jakobsen, Kleven & Zucman (QJE 2020) used the 1989 cut and 1997 repeal quasi-experimentally: reducing wealth taxes made wealth at the top grow substantially faster — top-wealth couples' wealth ~30% higher after ~30 years than under the tax; implied long-run elasticity of taxable wealth ~0.7 for the very wealthy (moderate, driven by both real saving and reporting). Despite full repeal, Denmark did NOT get runaway inequality — top 1% share ~20% of wealth (about half the US level). Stated repeal reasons: low revenue, avoidance/mobility fears, part of 1990s marginal-rate-cutting reforms.

Sources: <https://gabriel-zucman.eu/files/JJKZ2020.pdf> · <https://academic.oup.com/qje/article-abstract/135/1/329/5584349> · <https://www.nber.org/papers/w24371> · <https://microeconomicinsights.org/wealth-taxation-and-wealth-accumulation-theory-and-evidence-from-denmark/>

### Finland
*Status: repealed(2006) — effective 1 Jan 2006, under the Vanhanen I centre/SDP coalition*

**The tax:** Varallisuusvero — annual net wealth tax on individuals

**Rates & thresholds:** 0.9% on net taxable assets above €185,000 (rate/threshold cited at abolition); in the final year the rate had been cut to 0.8% with the threshold raised to €250,000. (Sources split between the 0.9%/€185k and 0.8%/€250k figures because the parameters changed in 2005 — both are the same tax at different dates.)

**How wealth is measured:** Narrow, favor-riddled base: listed securities entered at reduced 'taxation values' (~70% of market), housing at fiscal values below market, bank deposits largely exempt, business assets favorably valued — so effective rates varied enormously by asset class.

**Revenue:** Very low — one of the lowest-yield wealth taxes in the OECD, roughly 0.1% of total tax revenue near repeal (~€70–100m/yr order of magnitude; no precise final-year figure verified across two sources, flagging that).

**Effects & rulings:** Stated reasons: an EU report noted the reform was 'motivated by the fact that the tax had an unfair impact on enterprises'; competitiveness with Sweden/Norway; abolition was packaged with the 2005 corporate/dividend tax reform (corporate rate 29%→26%). No prominent post-repeal empirical study.

Sources: <https://www.wealthbriefing.com/html/article.php/finland-abolish-wealth-tax> · <https://www.cato.org/publications/commentary/why-europe-axed-its-wealth-taxes> · <https://www.wealthandpolicy.com/wp/EP6_PoliticsAndDesign.pdf> · <https://taxsummaries.pwc.com/finland/individual/other-taxes>

### France
*Status: repealed (2018) — replaced by the real-estate-only IFI*

**The tax:** Impôt de solidarité sur la fortune (ISF), 1989–2017 (predecessor IGF 1982–86, abolished 1987) — annual tax on TOTAL net wealth: financial assets, real estate, valuables, worldwide for residents.

**Rates & thresholds:** Final scale: 6 brackets, 0.5% to 1.5%. Liability triggered once net taxable wealth exceeded €1.3M, but tax then computed from €800,000 upward (0.5% €800k–1.3M; 0.7% €1.3–2.57M; 1% €2.57–5M; 1.25% €5–10M; 1.5% above €10M). Cap: ISF + income taxes limited to 75% of income (imposed by the Conseil constitutionnel).

**How wealth is measured:** Self-assessed market value at 1 January each year (taxpayer declares and values own assets — a chronic audit battleground). 30% abatement on the main residence. Major exemptions hollowed the base: 'biens professionnels' (business assets/controlling active shareholdings), antiques and artworks, most pension rights, partial exemptions for woodland and long-held employee shares.

**Revenue:** Final year 2017: €4.2bn net from spontaneous declarations (€5.07bn including €0.84bn of audit recoveries), ~358,000 households — roughly 0.4% of total French tax revenue.

**Effects & rulings:** Most-cited estimate (economist Eric Pichet, SSRN): ~€200bn cumulative capital flight since 1988 and a drag of ~0.2% of GDP per year; several hundred ISF-paying households emigrated annually in the 2000s–2010s (Pichet's 'about two taxpayers a day'). The Senate's own 2019 review and the government both cited capital flight, thin net yield after collection/avoidance costs, and disincentives to invest in French firms as reasons Macron scrapped it. Note: causality on emigration is contested — official evaluators call the flows 'small numbers'.

Sources: <https://www.senat.fr/rap/r19-042-1/r19-042-12.html> · <https://www.impots.gouv.fr/isf-annee-2017> · <https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1268381> · <https://www.french-property.com/guides/france/finance-taxation/taxation/wealth-tax> · <https://www.euronews.com/business/2026/07/24/why-are-frances-top-earners-leaving-and-where-are-they-going>

### Iceland
*Status: repealed(2006); temporarily reintroduced 2010–2014 as an emergency measure, then allowed to expire on schedule*

**The tax:** Eignarskattur (abolished end-2005/effective 2006); crisis-era auðlegðarskattur ('affluence tax') 2010–2014

**Rates & thresholds:** Old tax: 1.45% before 2003, cut to 0.6% in 2003, abolished from 2006. Temporary tax: 1.25% in 2010 above ISK 90m (single) / 120m (couples); raised to 1.5% in 2011 with thresholds lowered to ISK 75m/100m; a higher top bracket (2%) applied in the later years. Sunset honored at end-2014.

**How wealth is measured:** Broad base for the temporary tax: financial assets, business assets and real estate, self-assessed against tax-return data; IMF criticized it as 'effectively a second income tax on capital.'

**Revenue:** Temporary tax expected to raise ~ISK 3.5 billion/yr (IMF 2010), roughly 0.2% of GDP; the pre-2006 tax's yield was modest. OECD Revenue Statistics has no clean disaggregated series for Iceland's NWT.

**Effects & rulings:** Iceland is the base-rate exception that proves the rule: a wealth tax CAN be reintroduced — but only as an explicitly temporary post-crisis measure, at crisis rates, and it was still allowed to lapse on schedule with IMF blessing rather than made permanent. No strong evidence of capital flight during the 4-year window (capital controls were in place post-2008, which mattered).

Sources: <https://freedomandprosperity.org/2007/publications/the-iceland-tax-system-key-features-and-lessons-for-policy-makers/> · <https://www.wealthandpolicy.com/wp/EP7_OneOff.pdf> · <https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/ft/scr/2011/cr11138.pdf> · <https://www.newstatesman.com/business/economics/2012/08/what-can-iceland-teach-us-about-wealth-tax> · <https://conversableeconomist.com/2021/02/01/why-have-other-high-income-countries-dropped-wealth-taxes/>

### OECD cross-country (history, measurement infrastructure, arguments)
*Status: repealed in most of the OECD (12 countries in 1990 → 3-4 today); infrastructure and debate both current*

**The tax:** Reference frame for everything above. Repeal wave: Austria 1994, Germany 1997 (constitutional-court suspension 1995), Denmark 1997, Netherlands 2001, Finland/Iceland/Luxembourg 2006, Sweden 2007, France (broad ISF) 2018 — leaving Switzerland, Norway, Spain in Europe plus Colombia in the OECD. OECD's 2018 'Role and Design of Net Wealth Taxes' concluded there is a limited case for a net wealth tax ON TOP OF well-designed capital-income and inheritance taxes, but a stronger case where those are weak (the Swiss configuration).

**Rates & thresholds:** Historic repeal drivers were consistent (Perret 2021, Fiscal Studies; Tax Foundation): administrative cost, valuation of illiquid assets (private firms, art), capital flight, and disappointing revenue.

**How wealth is measured:** What changed since the 1990s — the measurement frontier: (1) CRS automatic bank-information exchange live since 2017-18 across 100+ jurisdictions; NBER w32714 (Boas, Johannesen, Kreiner, Larsen, Zucman) and the EU Tax Observatory's 10-year CRS review find automatic exchange closed a large share of the offshore tax gap (up to ~70% in the Danish setting), removing the 1990s' easiest evasion route. (2) Remaining gaps: real estate and crypto outside CRS (crypto's CARF exchange starts ~2027), closely held entities where 'third-party' reporting collapses into self-reporting, and art/collectibles with no third-party net at all. (3) Valuation practice where taxes exist: listed assets marked to market annually; real estate at administrative values below market; unlisted businesses by formula (Swiss practitioner method averaging capitalized earnings and net assets; Norway's fiscal net-asset value; Spain's highest-of book/nominal/capitalized) — formulas systematically undervalue, which is a deliberate liquidity concession and a horizontal-equity leak. (4) Reporting regimes span the whole spectrum: Norway near-fully third-party pre-filled; Spain self-assessment plus Modelo 720/CRS; Switzerland pure self-declaration with no domestic bank reporting — and measured evasion tracks that spectrum (Brülhart et al. Switzerland: large; Jakobsen et al. Denmark QJE 2020: moderate real responses; Seim Sweden: mostly evasion of self-reported assets).

**Revenue:** OECD category 4300 (recurrent taxes on net wealth), 2022: Switzerland 1.19% of GDP / 4.35% of total tax; Spain 0.19% / 0.51%; Norway ~0.4-0.6% / ~1.1-1.5%. OECD-wide, net wealth taxes are a rounding error outside Switzerland.

**Effects & rulings:** STANDARD ARGUMENTS, stated neutrally. FOR (Zucman, Saez, Piketty, EU Tax Observatory, Oxfam, Tax Justice Network, Patriotic Millionaires): the ultra-rich pay lower effective rates than ordinary taxpayers because returns compound untaxed in holding companies and unrealized gains escape income tax — a wealth-based minimum is the only instrument that reaches this; wealth concentration itself has political-economy costs; CRS/FATCA now make enforcement feasible in ways impossible during the 1990s repeal wave; revenue is meaningful at the very top ($200-250bn/yr globally). AGAINST (Tax Foundation, OECD 2018 cautions, Adam Smith Institute, business federations, Senate opponents in France, AG analysis in Washington): historically low revenue (0.2-1.2% of GDP even where it works) against high administration/compliance cost; annual valuation of private firms and art is arbitrary or avoidable; liquidity strain forces owners to strip dividends or sell stakes (core Norwegian critique); mobility/capital-flight responses are real (Norway's documented exodus; Brülhart's 43%-per-1pp reported-wealth elasticity); it double-taxes normal returns to saving, so taxing realized capital income and inheritances better targets the same base at lower distortion; legal fragility (German 1995 suspension, Dutch ECHR rulings, US direct-tax clause). MIDDLE (OECD 2018, Scheuer-Slemrod, Wealth Tax Commission): design dominates — high thresholds, third-party reporting, market-linked valuation, and exit taxes fix much of the historic failure, but at the price of narrowing the base to exactly the taxpayers most able to relocate.

Sources: <https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/the-role-and-design-of-net-wealth-taxes-in-the-oecd_9789264290303-en.html> · <https://taxfoundation.org/blog/wealth-taxes-in-the-oecd/> · <https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1475-5890.12278> · <https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w32714/w32714.pdf> · <https://taxobservatory.world/publication/from-tax-secrecy-to-tax-transparency-10-years-of-common-reporting-standard/> · <https://taxfoundation.org/research/all/eu/wealth-tax-impact/> · <https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Fpol.20200258> · <https://taxfoundation.org/data/all/eu/wealth-taxes-europe/>

### Sweden
*Status: repealed(2007)*

**The tax:** Förmögenhetsskatt — annual net wealth tax on individuals, levied 1911–2006, abolished with effect from 1 Jan 2007 by the incoming centre-right Reinfeldt coalition (following estate/gift tax repeal in 2004, done under the Social Democrats)

**Rates & thresholds:** At repeal: 1.5% on net wealth above SEK 1.5 million (single) / SEK 3.0 million (married couples/partners). Paid by only ~2.5% of taxpayers.

**How wealth is measured:** Household net wealth self-declared but heavily third-party reported (banks/brokers). Listed 'A-list' shares taxable at 80% of market value, but O-list (entrepreneur/controlling) shares and 'working capital' in active businesses were EXEMPT — so Kamprad-class fortunes escaped while middle-class housing and savings were hit. Real estate at tax-assessed value (~75% of market). This inverted-progressivity was a core stated complaint.

**Revenue:** ~SEK 4.5–5 billion/yr at repeal ≈ 0.16% of GDP and ~0.3% of total tax revenue (2006).

**Effects & rulings:** Capital flight was the stated motivator: the Swedish Tax Agency estimated >SEK 500 billion of assets illicitly moved offshore by the early 2000s; high-profile emigrations (Kamprad/IKEA to Switzerland, Rausing/Tetra Pak). Seim (AEJ: Economic Policy 2017) measured elasticity of taxable wealth of only 0.09–0.27 while the tax existed, concluding responses 'reflect evasion and avoidance rather than changes in saving' (about a third pure underreporting). Post-repeal: capital repatriation reported, but no measurable surge in real household saving (Du Rietz & Henrekson). Base-rate lesson: the tax died of a narrow, leaky base + offshore evasion, not of raising too much money.

## Suspended

### Germany
*Status: suspended(1997) — de facto repealed; the Vermögensteuergesetz was never formally repealed, it simply has not been levied since 1 Jan 1997*

**The tax:** Vermögensteuer — annual net wealth tax collected by the Länder on individuals and corporations; last levied for 1996

**Rates & thresholds:** Individuals: 1% (0.5% for business/agricultural-forestry assets), personal allowance DM 120,000 per family member. Corporations: 0.6%.

**How wealth is measured:** The fatal flaw and the reason for the court ruling: real estate was assessed at 'Einheitswerte' (unit values) frozen at 1 Jan 1964 levels, while financial assets were assessed at current market value — same 1% rate on wildly unequal effective bases. Federal Constitutional Court, 22 June 1995 (2 BvL 37/91), held this violated the equality principle (Art. 3 Grundgesetz) and gave the legislature until end-1996 to fix valuation; the Kohl government chose to let the tax lapse instead. The ruling also floated the (later abandoned, 2006) 'Halbteilungsgrundsatz' — total tax should not exceed roughly half of asset yield.

**Revenue:** DM 9 billion (€4.62 billion) in 1996, the final year ≈ roughly 1% of total tax revenue, ~0.25% of GDP. Non-trivial — Germany is the case where a wealth tax was killed by a court, not by revenue collapse.

**Effects & rulings:** No credible post-suspension behavioral study (suspension was nationwide and simultaneous, no control group). Administrative-cost estimates drove the no-revival decision: Finance Ministry (1996) ~3% of revenue collection cost; RWI (1984 data) up to 32% of revenue including compliance; DIW (2016) 4.4–8.2%. Repeated reintroduction attempts (SPD/Greens/Linke, 1997–2016, incl. a 2012 Länder draft) all failed, mainly over valuation cost and constitutional risk.

## Context & mechanics

### OECD (base-rate overview)
*Status: context: 12 OECD countries levied individual net wealth taxes in 1990; only 4 remained by 2017 (France, Norway, Spain, Switzerland); France converted its ISF to a real-estate-only IFI in 2018, leaving 3 true net wealth taxes among the 1990 cohort*

**The tax:** Recurrent taxes on individuals' net wealth (OECD Tax Policy Study No. 26, 2018). Repeal sequence: Austria 1994, Denmark 1997, Germany 1997 (court-forced suspension), Netherlands 2001 (replaced by Box-3 deemed-return income tax), Finland/Iceland/Luxembourg 2006, Sweden 2007, France narrowed 2018.

**Rates & thresholds:** Typical yields for repealed taxes: ~0.2% of GDP or less (Sweden 0.16%, Denmark ~0.1–0.2%, Finland ~0.1% of tax revenue) — the outliers were Austria (~0.5% GDP, mostly corporate-paid) and Germany (~1% of tax revenue, killed by a court not by yield). Survivors are the exception: Switzerland raises ~1% of GDP (~3.5% of tax revenue) from cantonal wealth taxes — but Switzerland has no capital gains tax on most private assets, so the wealth tax substitutes for one.

**How wealth is measured:** The recurring cause of death across every case: valuation. Outdated cadastral/unit values (Germany, Austria), exemptions for pensions, primary homes, family businesses and unlisted shares (Sweden, Finland, Denmark) hollowed the base, made effective rates uneven and often regressive at the very top, and pushed administration+compliance costs to a large fraction of the modest revenue. OECD's summary: repeals were 'justified by efficiency and administrative concerns and by the observation that net wealth taxes have frequently failed to meet their redistributive goals.'

**Revenue:** Cross-country pattern: with few exceptions revenues were very low (0.1–0.3% of GDP) against total government revenue of 40%+ of GDP.

**Effects & rulings:** Base-rate summary for how wealth taxes end: (1) most died legislatively under LOW-yield + high-admin-cost + capital-flight arguments, several under left-of-center governments (Denmark, Finland, Austria's SPÖ minister); (2) one died by constitutional court over unequal valuation (Germany 1995/97); (3) one was reintroduced only as a temporary crisis measure and sunset on schedule (Iceland); (4) one was narrowed to survive politically (France 2018 ISF→IFI); (5) none of the 8 repealers has reinstated a broad NWT as of 2026. Measured post-repeal behavior where studied: top wealth grows faster after repeal (Denmark, ~30% higher after ~30 years) but without US-style inequality explosion; during-tax responses were dominated by avoidance/evasion rather than real saving (Sweden, Seim 2017).

Sources: <https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/the-role-and-design-of-net-wealth-taxes-in-the-oecd_9789264290303-en.html> · <https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1475-5890.12278> · <https://taxfoundation.org/blog/wealth-taxes-in-the-oecd/> · <https://conversableeconomist.com/2021/02/01/why-have-other-high-income-countries-dropped-wealth-taxes/> · <https://www.economicsobservatory.com/why-did-some-earlier-wealth-taxes-fail-and-could-time-be-different>

### United States — Washington State
*Status: wealth tax proposed and stalled; 9.9% millionaires income tax enacted (2026) with repeal referendum pending*

**The tax:** Repeated bills (SB 5486 2023; HB 1319 / Senate companion 2025–26) for a ~1% annual tax on financial intangible assets (stocks, bonds, funds) above $50m–$250m thresholds — all died in committee, and an AG memo warned Gov. Ferguson the wealth tax was likely unconstitutional under WA's uniformity clause. The legislature instead passed ESSB 6346 (12 Mar 2026, signed 30 Mar 2026): a 9.9% tax on household income over $1 million, effective 2028 — income, not wealth, but squarely aimed at the same base. A repeal initiative is headed for the Nov 2026 ballot; Ferguson is campaigning against repeal.

**Rates & thresholds:** Failed wealth-tax bills: 1% on intangibles above $50m (Senate) / $100m (House); earlier versions $250m. Enacted: 9.9% on income > $1m/yr from 1 Jan 2028.

**How wealth is measured:** Wealth-tax bills taxed mere ownership of listed intangibles (easy to value) precisely to dodge the illiquid-asset problem; critics noted this makes the base extremely mobile.

**Revenue:** Prospective only; millionaires tax first collections due 2029.

**Effects & rulings:** Demonstrates the recurring US state pattern: annual wealth taxes on intangibles fail on legal/mobility grounds and morph into high-earner income taxes. Maine and Minnesota are also advancing tax-the-rich measures (Foley & Lardner survey).
